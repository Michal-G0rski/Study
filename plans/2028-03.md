# March 2028 — the last month: build it, break it, write it down

Month 18 of [the plan](../README.md). **15 h/week: 2.5 h/day, Monday to Saturday, Sunday off.**

Scope only — what to read, what to build, what has to exist by Sunday. Setup and tooling are deliberately not here. Standing rules are in [plans/README.md](README.md).

Four clean weeks, sixty hours, no holidays. Easter is mid-April this year, so March runs full to the end.

The last month opens no new large source. It is a month for finishing and for showing the work — the difference between eighteen months of study and eighteen months of a portfolio someone will hire from. Four things: a web application you build and then break yourself, the side-channel reading the plan has been pointing at since July, a hardware teardown written up, and one honest attempt at a real CVE. Then the review that closes the whole thing.

## Sources in use this month

- Your own tools, mostly. A web app of your building, and the [Web Security Academy](https://portswigger.net/web-security) skills from Stage 2 turned back on it.
- [ChipWhisperer](https://chipwhisperer.readthedocs.io/) — **SCA101: Introduction to Power Analysis Attacks**, the side-channel work CS:APP chapter 6 was preparing since July.
- A real device you own, for the teardown — and whatever firmware or software you decide is worth a genuine vulnerability report.

> On ChipWhisperer without the hardware: SCA101's early labs ship in a **SIMULATED** variant alongside the hardware one, so the power-analysis fundamentals — the leakage model, the correlation attack on AES — can be done with no board on the desk. That is the scope here. Fault injection (the FAULT101 course) and the labs that need real traces are the natural next step and they need the ChipWhisperer hardware; they are post-plan, like Arch2001 and the System Security kernel modules. What March buys is the concept, proven in simulation: that a chip leaks its secrets through its power line, and that the cache-timing paragraph you wrote in July was the same idea in software.

> On the CVE attempt: the deliverable is the *attempt and its writeup*, not a published CVE. Finding a real, novel vulnerability in eighteen months is possible and not promised. What is promised is that you go through the whole motion once — pick a target, find something, confirm it is real, and write a responsible disclosure — because doing it once is the difference between "I could report a bug" and "I have." If you find nothing exploitable, the writeup is "here is what I looked at, here is why it held." That is still the deliverable.

> Nothing new opens because the plan is honest that eighteen months ends mid-curriculum. pwn.college's System Security is not finished, ROP Emporium's harder architectures are not exhausted, ChipWhisperer's fault work is untouched. That is by design, and the [accounting](README.md#what-eighteen-months-actually-holds) says so. This month is not for cramming the unfinished in; it is for making the finished visible.

## Rules for a week that goes wrong

The [standing rules](README.md) apply. Three things specific to the last month:

- **Publishing beats finishing.** A rough writeup on the blog is worth more than a perfect one in a draft folder. The whole month is about what a stranger can see; unpublished work is invisible work.
- **The CVE attempt is the motion, not the medal.** Do not let a fruitless hunt eat the review. If nothing gives by Tuesday, write "here is what held and why" and move on. The point was to go through disclosure once, and a clean negative result written up honestly is a real security artifact.
- **Do not cram the unfinished in.** The plan ends mid-curriculum on purpose. Spending the last week racing through half of System Security's kernel module produces nothing you can show and undoes the point of the review. Finish visibly; leave the rest for the list.
